Improving the method for calculating 
carbon emissions from waste incineration: 
confirmed with carbon-14 testing of flue gas
Yifei Ma1, Pinjing He1,2, Fan Lü1,2 and Hua Zhang1,2* 
Abstract 
An improved method was developed to calculate direct emissions from seven municipal solid waste incinera-
tion plants by adjusting the physical compositions of waste by invoking the proportion of co-incinerated waste 
and the bottom ash yield. The fossil carbon fractions of the waste components were determined by carbon-14 (14C) 
testing. Based on the improved method, direct emissions were in the range of 222–610 kg  CO2-eq/t waste, cor-
responding to reductions of 3.4–221 kg  CO2-eq/t waste compared with the method without waste composition 
adjustment. The 14C contents of the flue gas before and after gas cleaning were tested to validate the improved 
method, and indicated fossil  CO2 emissions of 249–446 and 233–405 kg  CO2-eq/t waste, respectively. The direct emis-
sions obtained by the improved method were closer to the results of 14C testing, due to more accurate estimations 
of the actual waste composition. The method was further combined with a life cycle analysis of the waste incinera-
tion process, obtaining total carbon emissions in the range from –33.2 to 483 kg  CO2-eq/t waste. The findings provide 
a new means of accurately calculating carbon emissions from waste incineration.
Highlights 
• Co-incinerated waste and bottom ash yield were used to adjust waste composition.
• Fossil carbon contents of the waste and flue gas were determined by 14C testing.
• The improved method reduced fossil  CO2 emissions by 3.4–221 kg  CO2-eq/t waste.
• Life cycle GHG emissions from waste incineration was –33.2–483 kg  CO2-eq/t waste.
Keywords Direct emissions, Fossil carbon fraction, Accelerator mass spectrometry, Co-incineration, Bottom ash, 
Waste composition adjustment

1 Introduction
Improving the carbon accounting system is a necessary 
procedure to achieve carbon neutrality (Wu et al. 2022). 
In the area of solid waste management, municipal solid 
waste incineration (MSWI) is one of the most promising 
pathways to reduce greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions, 
as incineration facilities provide both electricity genera -
tion and an alternative to landfilling (Liao et al. 2023; Fei 
et al. 2024). As the waste composition and energy mix in 
China have changed over the years, the carbon benefits 
of waste incineration have incurred a downward trend 
(China Academy of Environmental Planning 2023; Han 
et  al. 2023; Zhang et  al. 2023). Accurate accounting of 
carbon emissions from MSWI is now widely scrutinized 
(Zhao et al. 2022; Xiao et al. 2023).
The accounting methods for carbon emissions from 
MSWI include the method established by the intergov -
ernmental panel on climate change (IPCC) (Eggles -
ton et  al. 2006), life cycle assessment (LCA) (Liao et  al. 
2023), the balance method (Schwarzböck et  al. 2016), 
the carbon-14 ( 14C) method (Lee et  al. 2021), and the 
clean development mechanism (Yao et al. 2019). Recent 
studies have primarily focused on the IPCC method and 
LCA. The IPCC method employs emission factors and 
formulas to calculate direct emissions from fossil compo-
nents in waste. The LCA method, which further utilizes 
operational data from MSWI plants, identifies both indi -
rect emissions from energy and material consumption 
as well as the offset avoided emissions from electricity 
generation. Due to abundant operational data and the 
existence of product life cycle databases, the calculation 
of indirect emissions and avoided emissions is typically 
straightforward. However, the calculation of direct emis -
sions is more complex, as determining the fossil carbon 
fraction (FCF) in waste often involves complicated data 
research and experimental work. To date, such determi -
nation of FCF has involved sorting waste into more uni -
form components (paper, plastic, glass, metal, textiles, 
etc.) and analyzing the total carbon content (TC) and 
the distribution of FCFs among these components (Chen 
et al. 2020).
Nevertheless, due to a lack of visual identifiability, a 
significant fraction is often overlooked due to small par -
ticle size, with the most significant impact being on the 
content of inert waste such as dust and debris. In recent 
studies, the total ash contents of waste components in 
industrial analysis often appeared to be low (Malakah -
mad et al. 2017; Pérez et al. 2018; Zhao et al. 2022), but 
the actual yields of bottom ash in MSWI plants were 
high (20%–30%) (Sabbas et al. 2003; Li et al. 2020; Chen 
et al. 2023). This implies that the ash content in the waste 
was underestimated, while the contents of other compo -
nents, such as plastics, which have a significant impact 
on carbon emissions, were overestimated. Furthermore, 
changes in waste management policies have led to altera -
tions in the reality of MSWI. The implementation of 
waste sorting policies has resulted in the diversion of a 
significant amount of food waste from municipal solid 
waste (MSW) (Shi et  al. 2024), leading to a substantial 
reduction in the waste sent to MSWI plants. Meanwhile, 
the rapid increase of MSWI plants in recent years has 
led to a challenge of their underutilization. In response 
to this situation, MSWI plants have shifted towards the 
co-incineration of various types of waste, including

non-hazardous industrial solid waste, disinfected medi -
cal waste, dried sludge, residues from food waste treat -
ment, and so on (Zhao et al. 2021; Ying et al. 2023; Guo 
et al. 2023; Chu et al. 2024). However, the components of 
these co-incinerated wastes are typically not considered 
in MSWI carbon emission accounting (Zhao et al. 2022; 
Liao et al. 2023; Han et al. 2023).
Currently, research efforts on the FCFs of various waste 
components primarily rely on the default values provided 
by IPCC guidelines (Kourkoumpas et al. 2015; Liu et al. 
2017; Wang et al. 2017), which may not accurately reflect 
the characteristics of China’s waste. Studies have indi -
cated differences between the IPCC default values and 
the actual FCFs (Kang et al. 2017a; Liu et al. 2024). There-
fore, to improve the reliability of accounting results, it is 
necessary to determine the FCF of each component in 
waste. The 14C test is an effective method for quantifying 
the fossil carbon content in waste. Theoretically, mod -
ern biomass has 100% radiocarbon content, in contrast 
to fossil fuels, which contain virtually no radiocarbons 
(American Society for Testing and Materials 2024). The 
biogenic fraction correlates directly with the 14C content, 
allowing for its precise determination. Consequently, the 
14C method adeptly differentiates between fossil and bio -
genic carbon sources (Kang et  al. 2020). This approach 
ensures the accurate assessment of fossil carbon in waste. 
Additionally, employing 14C testing to determine the 
concentrations of fossil  CO2 in flue gas is a more pre -
cise method for directly calculating carbon emissions 
from waste incineration (Wang et al. 2022). The flue gas 
from incineration is the most suitable target for validat -
ing direct carbon emissions (Kim et al. 2016; Kang et al. 
2018), because the homogeneous nature of flue gas 
allows for avoidance of the issue raised by unrepresenta -
tive sampling of heterogeneous solid waste (Mohn et al. 
2012). Nevertheless, the challenges of flue gas sampling 
and the expense of 14C testing often discourage research-
ers from conducting extensive sampling and tests.
Therefore, this study proposes an improved method 
for determining direct emissions from MSWI plants by 
invoking the adjusted physical composition of waste. 
The improvement comprises two steps: step 1, adjust -
ing the physical composition of incinerated waste based 
on the quantities of co-incinerated waste materials; step 
2, adjusting the physical composition from step 1 based 
on the actual bottom ash production. Additionally, 14C 
testing was adopted to determine the FCF of each com -
ponent in waste, providing carbon emission account -
ing parameters that align with the current situation in 
China, further improving the method. To validate the 
results, direct emissions from waste incineration under 
eight different conditions were determined through 14C 
testing of flue gas. The direct emissions determined by 
the improved method were then combined with a full 
life cycle inventory analysis to evaluate the current level 
of carbon emissions from MSWI in China.
2  Method
2.1  MSWI plants
Seven typical MSWI plants in Shanghai (plants A–E), 
Jiangsu (plant F), and Hebei province (plant G) in 
China were selected as study cases, with eight sets of 
operating conditions. For plant G, two sets of work -
ing conditions were operative, namely incineration of 
MSW alone, denoted as G(1), and co-incineration of 
MSW with 10% polyester textiles, denoted as G(2). The 
three regions selected, with different climatic and eco -
nomic conditions, level of development and population 
densities (179,907, 144,390 and 56,995 CNY for per 
capita gross regional product,  and 3950, 794 and 393 
persons/km 2 for population density in 2022 in Shang -
hai, Jiangsu, and Hebei province, respectively), ensured 
that the waste samples were broadly representative. The 
waste management practices in the three regions, in 
turn, represent the typical waste segregation patterns 
and levels today. At the MSWI plants A–E sites, MSW 
was collected separately as hazardous waste, recycla -
bles, food waste and residual waste, with residual waste 
ultimately being sent to MSWI plants. At the locations 
of MSWI plant F and MSWI plant G, MSW was col -
lected separately as hazardous waste, recyclables and 
other waste, so the residual waste sent to the MSWI 
plant had a high food waste content. The incineration 
sizes of the MSWI plants in the three regions ranged 
from 750 t/d to 6,000 t/d, and the incineration and pol -
lutant management technologies used covered typical 
waste incineration scenarios. During the period from 
November 2022 to November 2023, samples and opera -
tional data were collected from the MSWI plants. The 
main parameters and system indicators of the investi -
gated plants are shown in Table 1 .

2.2  Sample collection and characterization
Besides MSW, other types of waste are co-incinerated in 
the plants. Therefore, sample collection and analysis of 
the physical compositions of incoming waste materials 
at the plants became more complicated, covering MSW 
as well as blended industrial solid waste, medical waste, 
sludge, and food waste treatment residues. MSW, as the 
bulk of the incoming waste, was sampled in the same 
way as in the study by Liao et al. (2023). The amounts of 
other co-incinerated waste materials were obtained from 
the operational data. Typical co-incinerated waste com -
ponents included polyester textiles and rubber in indus -
trial solid waste; syringes, surgical drape, surgical gowns, 
and medical pads in medical waste; organic solid residues 
and biogas residues in food waste treatment residues; and 
sludge (Table 2).
Each typical component of the MSW and co-inciner -
ated waste materials was dried and crushed separately, 
and then passed through a 100-mesh sieve to ensure 
homogeneity of the samples. The TC of the samples was 
determined by elemental analysis (Vario EL Cube, Ele -
mentar, Germany). The FCFs of the samples were deter -
mined by accelerator mass spectrometry (AMS). Each 
solid waste sample with an absolute carbon content of 
200–1000 µg was placed in a quartz tube to which an 
excess of copper oxide particles had been added, which 
was then connected to a vacuum system. The quartz tube 
containing the sample was then sealed and placed in a 
muffle furnace at 850 °C for 1 h, during which the organic 
carbon was oxidized to  CO2. The gaseous products were 
then collected for 14C testing, the protocol for which is 
described in Sect. 2.4.
2.3  Flue gas sampling and  CO2 analysis
The method ASTM D7459-08 (American Society for 
Testing and Materials 2016) defines specific procedures 
for the collection of gas samples from stationary emis -
sion sources for subsequent laboratory determination of 
the ratio of fossil carbon to biogenic carbon according to 
method ASTM D6866-22 (American Society for Testing 
and Materials 2024). In this study, a sampling method 
was established with reference to ASTM D7459-08, 
and flue gas samples were collected from seven MSWI 
plants operated under eight sets of conditions. Two sam -
pling points were selected at each MSWI plant, namely 
the boiler economizer outlet and the chimney outlet, 
to determine the total  CO2 and fossil  CO2 concentra -
tions in flue gas before and after the gas cleaning system, 
respectively.
Figure 1 shows a schematic diagram of the flue gas col -
lection and monitoring. A primary filter made of stainless 
steel was set up at the back end of the 1 m long sampling 
probe to prioritize the removal of particles larger than 0.1 
mm in the flue gas. The flue gas was then passed through 
a tracer line maintained at 180 °C to avoid losses of its 
components due to condensation. A specific conden -
sation system (PSS-20, M&C TechGroup, Germany), 
equipped with a polytetrafluoroethylene gas filter to 
remove particles larger than 2 μm, was used to remove 
moisture from the flue gas. The condensation system

2.4  14C test
Flue gas samples and gaseous products from solid waste 
components were purified and graphitized prior to 14C 
testing by AMS. Gas samples were transferred to a vac -
uum system at a flow rate of 200 mL/min, in which  H2O 
and  CO2 were sequentially frozen by passage through a 
liquid nitrogen cold trap (–196 ℃), while other impurity 
gases were pumped out of the system through a vacuum 
line. The  H2O was then removed by an ethanol-liquid 
nitrogen water-cooling trap (–78 ℃ to –90 ℃) to obtain 
purified  CO2. The purified  CO2 was then transferred to 
a glass reaction casing, the outer tube of which was filled 
with Zn and  TiH2 powders, and the inner tube was filled 
with Fe powder. After flame-sealing under vacuum, the 
glass casing was heated in a muffle furnace.  CO2 under-
went a reduction reaction on the surface of the Fe powder 
in the inner tube after successive stepwise heating at 500 
°C for 3 h and at 550 °C for 4 h, and was finally reduced 
to graphite. The graphite sample was then pressed into an 
aluminum support prior to analysis.
The 14C levels of the graphite samples were measured 
by AMS (0.5 MV, NEC, USA) and the errors in the 14C 
measurements were within 3‰ of the 14C concentration 
of the samples. The biological carbon fractions (BCFs) 
of the samples were expressed in terms of modern car -
bon fraction f M,sample (unit: pMC). The test data were

2.7  Total GHG emissions associated with waste 
incineration
When the LCA method is used, the total GHG emission 
 (GHGtotal) from waste incineration includes the direct 
emission  (GHGdirect) due to fossil  CO2 emission, indirect 
emission  (GHGindirect), and avoided emission  (GHGavoid), 
calculated according to Eq. (8).
Indirect emissions in MSWI plants are associated with 
auxiliary fuel and electricity consumption, flue gas clean -
ing, leachate treatment, and fly ash/bottom ash treatment 
and disposal.  GHGindirect is determined by multiplying 
the consumption of energy and materials involved in 
the indirect emissions process with the relevant prod -
uct carbon footprint factors. The MSWI plants studied 
here were located in three different geographic locations 
and had varying waste collection and transportation 
routes. Considering that emissions from the collection 
and transportation phase are extremely low compared 
to the overall carbon emission level of waste incineration 
(Pfadt-Trilling et  al. 2021; Zhao et  al. 2022), they were 
excluded from the indirect emission accounting in this 
study. Avoided emissions from waste incineration plants 
were embodied in energy recovery in terms of electric -
ity, determined by multiplying the amount of electricity 
generated from waste incineration by the carbon emis -
sion factor of the electricity in which the MSWI plant 
is located. Due to variations in electricity generation 
efficiency and regional carbon emission factors for elec -
tricity, different MSWI plants achieved varying degrees 
of GHG emission reduction. The life cycle inventory of

3  Results and discussion
3.1  Influence of FCF on fossil  CO2 emissions by Method I
The FCFs of the MSW components obtained by 14C test-
ing showed characteristic differences from the IPCC 
default values (Table  2). As a result, the direct carbon 
emissions from waste incineration calculated accord -
ing to Method I using these two sets of data also showed 
some differences (Fig. 2).
Plastic waste is the main contributor to fossil  CO2 
emissions from MSW due to its high proportion therein 
and high FCF. The analyzed FCF of plastic in MSW was 
90.52% ± 0.81%, somewhat lower than the IPCC default 
value of 100%, leading to a significant difference in the 
fossil  CO2 emissions from plastic incineration (Fig.  2). 
For textiles, significant differences in FCF between cotton 
and polyester textiles were found, 0% and 93.6% ± 0.02%, 
respectively, whereas the IPCC stipulated an average 
value of 20% based on surveys and expert opinion. In 
order to provide an FCF applicable to textiles in Chinese 
MSW, a value of 62.4% ± 0.01% was calculated for mixed 
textiles therein, based on the ratio of pure natural cot -
ton textiles to polyester textiles produced in China (1:2) 
(China Association of Circular Economy 2023). In the 
case of a higher proportion of mixed textiles in MSW 
(plants A–E and plant F), the fossil  CO2 emissions from 
textiles evaluated using the analyzed FCF and IPCC 
default values were significantly different. In plant G(2), 
in which 10% polyester textiles are co-incinerated, the 
fossil  CO2 emission from the polyester textiles fraction 
using the analyzed FCF (157 kg  CO2-eq/t waste) was 
123 kg  CO2-eq/t waste higher than that using the IPCC 
default, making it the main carbon emission contributor. 
The analyzed FCF results for food waste (3.67% ± 0.95%), 
paper (3.51% ± 0.69%), and wood fractions (0.64% ± 0.46%) 
were slightly higher than the IPCC defaults, and the ana -
lyzed FCF for nappies (73.11% ± 7.28%) was significantly 
higher than the IPCC default. However, due to the low 
FCF values of the first three fractions and the low pro -
portion of nappies in MSW, they did not lead to a signifi -
cant increase in fossil  CO2 emissions.

3.3  Validation of the improved method based on flue gas 
analysis
The flue gas samples collected from MSWI plants A–G 
were subjected to 14C testing to determine the proportion 
of fossil  CO2 in the total  CO2. Successful development 
and implementation of the 14C method for measuring 
FCFfg from MSWI plants was demonstrated by Mohn 
et  al. (2008). Validation was performed by the balance 
method (Fellner et al. 2007), as a completely independent 
method, at three different MSWI plants, and the results 
of both methods were in good agreement. Furthermore, 
the 14C test method for flue gas has been successfully 
applied for the measurement of  CO2 emissions from 
the incineration of sludge (Kang et al. 2017b), industrial 
waste (Kang et al. 2020), and solid refuse fuel (Lee et al. 
2021).
In Fig. 5a, the FCFfg (proportion of fossil  CO2 in the total 
 CO2) in untreated flue gas and treated flue gas are seen 
to be 44.64%–53.19% and 44.54%–53.12%, respectively, 
in general agreement with the test results of (Mohn et al. 
2012). and (Palstra and Meijer 2010). The  CO2 concentra-
tions in the flue gas and other data in Table S3 were used 
to evaluate emissions of fossil  CO2 from untreated flue 
gas (Method IV) and treated flue gas (Method V), which 
resulted in ranges of 249–445 kg  CO2-eq/t waste and 
233–405 kg  CO2-eq/t waste, respectively (Fig.  5b). The 
t-test showed that there was no significant difference in 
the results of fossil  CO2 emissions determined by Method 
IV and Method V (p  = 0.393). The flue gas cleaning sys -
tems of the MSWI plants did not significantly change 
the relative proportions of fossil  CO2 in the total  CO2, 
but they affected the total amount of  CO2 in the flue gas. 
After treatment, the  CO2 concentrations in the flue gases 
of MSWI plants A–G were reduced by 0.26%–2.14%. This 
was due to  CO2 fixation by Ca(OH) 2 and other alkaline 
substances, the dissolution of  CO2 in water, and the dilu-
tion of  CO2 by air infiltration in the flue gas system.

3.4  Life cycle GHG emissions by the improved method
The total climate change contributions of MSWI plants 
A–G are given in Fig.  7. Direct emissions are based on 
the results of fossil  CO2 emission obtained from Method 
III, indirect emission accounting is based on operational 
data from MSWI plants, and avoided emissions are based 
on electricity substitution. Electricity exchange is based 
on the marginal mixes in the area in which the incinera -
tion plant is located, amounting to 0.420 kg  CO2-eq/kWh 
for plants A–E, 0.695 kg  CO2-eq/kWh for plant F, and 
1.092 kg  CO2-eq/kWh for plants G(1) and G(2). The total 
carbon emissions from all MSWI plants ranged from 
–33.2 to 483 kg  CO2-eq/t waste, with large discrepancies 
between different MSWI plants.
In all of the studied MSWI plants, indirect emissions 
were in the range  of 15.59–40.39 kg  CO2-eq/t waste, 
corresponding to only 3.80%–9.06% of the direct emis -
sions. The main concern when considering carbon emis -
sions from waste incineration remains direct and avoided 
emissions. The improved method of direct emission 
accounting shows that it is mainly affected by the physi -
cal composition of the waste, especially the content of 
fossil source waste. Better waste management and recy -
cling of fossil source waste with high recycling value, 
such as plastics, may yield better carbon emission reduc -
tion benefits.
The avoided emissions of MSWI plants A–G ranged 
from –299 to –115 kg  CO2-eq/t waste. It is worth noting 
that the GHG emission reduction from electricity gen -
eration does not depend entirely on the efficiency of the 
MSWI plant. The carbon emission factor of the electric -
ity in the area in which the incineration plant is situated 
is a non-negligible factor. MSWI plant D had the high -
est electricity generation efficiency, 494 kWh/t waste, 
but its carbon emission reduction through electricity 
generation was only 207 kg  CO2-eq/t waste, much lower 
than that of MSWI plant G(2) (–299 kg  CO2-eq/t waste), 
which had an electricity generation efficiency of only 274 
kWh/t waste. This is due to the fact that the carbon emis-
sion factor for electricity in MSWI plant D was 0.420 kg 
 CO2-eq/kWh, whereas that in MSWI plant G(2) was 2.6 
times higher (1.092 kg  CO2-eq/kWh).
The improved Method III reveals a certain reduction 
in direct carbon emissions from MSWI compared to the 
generic Method I. Due to the high carbon emission fac -
tor, the avoided GHG emission in some areas can offset 
the direct and indirect GHG emissions to a great extent, 
and even achieve negative net carbon emissions (–35.1 
kg  CO2-eq/t waste in plant G[1]), making waste incinera -
tion a carbon sink. However, as the energy mix becomes 
cleaner, the carbon reduction benefits of waste incinera -
tion will diminish, making waste incineration a carbon 
source. Therefore, reducing direct carbon emissions 
by increasing the recycling value of fossil waste, and 
increasing emission reductions from energy utilization 
by improving the efficiency of electricity generation or 
developing other energy-efficient thermal energy utiliza -
tion methods, such as combined heat and power, should 
be the direction of future efforts to effectively reduce 
GHG emissions associated with waste incineration.
4  Conclusions
This study proposed an improved method for calculating 
direct emissions from waste incineration on the basis of 
the common method (Method I) and their validation by 
the method of 14C testing of flue gas. The physical com -
position was adjusted by considering the co-incinerated 
waste materials (Method II) and the actual bottom ash 
yield (Method III). Furthermore, life cycle carbon emis -
sions from waste incineration were evaluated by taking 
into account actual operational data from MSWI plants.
14C testing was applied to obtain the FCFs of different 
types of solid waste materials that are more in line with 
current Chinese waste characteristics. The fossil carbon 
contents of plastics, polyester textiles, and mixed textiles, 
which play a major role in carbon emissions from waste 
incineration, showed significant deviations from the 
IPCC default values, being evaluated as 90.52% ± 0.81%, 
93.6% ± 0.02%, and 62.4% ± 0.01%, respectively. Under 
the eight different incineration conditions, Method III 
yielded fossil  CO2 emissions of 222–610 kg  CO2-eq/t 
waste, corresponding to reductions of 3.4–221 kg 
 CO2-eq/t waste compared to Method I. Flue gas was ana-
lyzed to verify Method III. The fossil  CO2 emissions were 
249–445 kg  CO2-eq/t waste before flue gas treatment 
(Method IV) and 233–405 kg  CO2-eq/t waste after flue 
gas treatment (Method V). After fully considering the 
uncertainties in the calculation parameters, the results of 
improved Method III were closer to the validation values 
than in the case of Method I, and should further improve 
the accuracy of the calculated results of carbon emissions 
from waste incineration. Life cycle carbon emissions 
from MSWI plants, taking into account operational data, 
ranged from –33.2 to 483 kg  CO2-eq/t waste. That is to 
say, some incineration plants are currently able to achieve 
negative net carbon emissions, due to the lower propor -
tion of fossil-source waste materials and the higher local 
carbon emission factor for electricity.
The results of this study should contribute to more accu-
rate accounting of carbon emissions from waste incin -
eration and provide data and methodological support for 
GHG emission accounting for waste management. Data 
of this study on the fossil carbon contents of solid waste 
samples and waste incineration flue gases derived from 14C 
tests should greatly enrich the database of waste manage-
ment and the life cycle inventory of GHG emissions.

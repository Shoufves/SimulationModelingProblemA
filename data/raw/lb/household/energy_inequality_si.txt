Supplementary Note 1: Definitions of energy transition, energy cost, energy
burden, energy poverty, and energy inequality
In this study, the term ‘energy transition’ was defined as the energy shift from the
traditional solid fuels to gas fuels and electricity during our research period. Note that
if a household has a convenient access to clean energy driven by energy transition
policies and actions, but concurrently use both of traditional solid-and clean fuels, we
also regarded the household undergoing energy transition because it also fairly
represents a special group. If we excluded it from the whole samples, the household
energy cost and its related energy burden are likely to be overestimated.
   Energy cost in this study was measured by how much a household spends on energy
bills per year, while ‘energy burden’ was defined as the percentage of household income
spent on energy bills. Based on this definition, existing studies have developed a variety
of thresholds to assess the magnitude of energy burden. For example, the United
Kingdom (UK) has officially incorporated energy poverty formally into its government
programs since 2000, applying an energy burden metric to assess whether households
are facing energy poverty. This metric has evolved from a simple ratio of household
income and energy expenditures (greater than 10%) to one that incorporates building
efficiency ratings and average incomes in the community2,3. Meanwhile, similar metrics
have been developed in member countries of the European Union (EU). In the US,
‘energy poor’ households commonly refer to those with an expenditure of greater than
6% of household income on meeting energy costs4-7, given energy expenditures should
not be greater than 20% of housing expenses, which themselves should not exceed 30%
of household income7. Moreover, Cook and Shah (2018) distinguished between ‘energy
stressed’ households with energy burdens of 4%–7%, ‘energy burdened’ households
with 7%–10% energy burdens, and ‘energy impoverished’ households with energy
burdens greater than 10%8. In short, there lacks a universal and unified threshold or
criterion for energy poverty assessment, however, the threshold of 10% is relatively
more common across the world9. Therefore, here we categorize households as living in
‘energy poverty’ when they spend more than 10% of their household income on energy
costs.
   The concept of ‘Energy inequality’ has been defined from a multidimensional
perspective in previous literature4,10-13. In this study, we defined it from two
perspectives, energy cost-based inequality and energy burden-based inequality. The
former can be quantitively estimated using Gini index, while the latter was discussed
and analyzed by comparison between urban and rural households across different
regions or income-groups.
Supplementary Note 2: Energy transition actions in China from 2013 to 2017
With the implementation of the toughest-ever clean air policy in China, Air Pollution
Prevention and Control Action Plan (APCAP) 14, a new campaign that substitute
residential solid fuels (coal, biomass) with clean fuels such as gas and electricity has
been launched by China’s central and local governments since 2013. In this regard, the

                                          14 / 19
year 2013 also featured a milestone: the residential coal-switch policy’s transformation
from a local pilot program to a large-scale regional application. These measures mainly
include decreasing the penetrations of direct coal-burning by replacing coal with natural
gas and electricity, with support of subsidizing household natural gas consumption and
constructing necessary infrastructures. Meanwhile, China’s central government
proposed an ambitious goal for Beijing, Tianjin, and 26 other municipalities in the
North, namely the clean heating rate should increase to 50% by 2019 and 70% by 2021,
in particular, it was planned that 60% of rural coal-using households will shift to clean
fuels by 202115. Along with the full implication of these measures, by the end of 2017,
energy consumption in 6 million households in China switched from coal to electricity
and natural gas16.
   In addition, as the socioeconomic development, China has also witnessed a rapid
energy transition in residential sector during 2011-201716,17. Here, based on the valid
data from 11,134-household survey in 2012, 12,946-household survey in 2014, 13,261-
household survey in 2016, and 12,418-household survey in 2018, we found that the
share of households using wood and crop residues decreased from 33.6%% in 2011 to
31.3% in 2013. Since 2013, the strict implementation of APCAP has led to a
considerable drop in the share of households using wood and crop residues, from 31.3%
to 26.7% in 2015, and 22.9% in 2017. Meanwhile, although the share of households
using gas increased obviously during 2011-2013, the growth rate became higher during
2013-2015, and 2015-2017, with the wide implementation of energy transition policy.
In addition, as we observed, the immediate effect of energy transition policy was more
significant in rural areas. These findings indicate the massive impacts of the APCAP
and other energy transition measures on the residential energy pattern mix in the short
term.
Supplementary Note 3: Evaluating the robustness of Gini index by comparing with
Theil and Atkinson coefficients
Among a variety of inequality indices, the Lorenz curve and the Gini index are one of
the most widely used analytical tools in the economics literature. There have been
several studies to measure inequalities in the energy-related fields 18-20. To assess the
robustness of the Gini index, this study also applied the Theil and Atkinson indices to
quantify the energy cost-based inequality trends. As shown in Supplementary Figure 7,
when employing the three indexes to quantify the household fuel cost and income
inequalities, their patterns and trends highly agreed with each other, indicating the
robustness of the Gini index in this study.
Supplementary Note 4: Effect assessment of rapid energy transition on energy
cost-based inequality at regional level
Considering the missing of specific household’s address at the city-level due to privacy
concerns, province-year fixed-effect model was employed to reveal the effect of rapid
energy transition on provincial energy inequality. Here, we used lnGit (representing the
Gini index for i province in t year) as the dependent variable, and a dummy variable
Pol_intensity was introduced to represent the energy transition campaign intensity at

                                         15 / 19
the provincial level.
   The Air Pollution Prevention and Control Action Plan was promulgated at the end of
2013 by the State Council of the People's Republic of China. Considering that it would
take some time to further come up with plans and implement detailed actions for local
governments, we assumed that the energy transition policies had a limited effect on the
household’s energy transition in 2013, thus all regions were assigned 0 in 2013.
Meanwhile, according to the Air Pollution Prevention and Control Action Plan, Beijing-
Tianjin-Hebei, Yangtze River Delta (including Shanghai, Jiangsu, Zhejiang), and Pearl
River Delta (Guangdong) were regarded as the major targeted regions to replace coal
with electricity, natural gas supply and the use of non-fossil energy. Thus, we assigned
1 to these seven cities or provinces, while 0 to the other provinces in 2015. In February
2017, the Clean Winter Heating Plan in Northern China was initiated and implemented
to accelerate the adoption of the clean energy for heating using in the Northern China.
Besides Beijing-Tianjin-Hebei, this plan also covered Shanxi, Shandong, and Henan.
Thus, we assigned 1 to these ten cities or provinces in 2017, including Beijing-Tianjin-
Hebei, Yangtze River Delta (including Shanghai, Jiangsu, Zhejiang), Pearl River Delta
(Guangdong), Shanxi, Shandong, and Henan, and the remaining fifteen provinces were
assigned 0. In addition, by referring to a study of Tao et al., (2019), two indicators
associated with households’ income (Income) and regional climate conditions (Climate)
were also introduced into the model in their logarithmic form. The definitions of these
independent variables and regression results are presented in Supplementary Table 3.
   The results show that both indicators of energy transition policy intensity
(Pol_intensity) and household income (lnIncome) were negatively associated with the
Gini index, indicating that rapid energy transition under government’s push will reduce
energy inequality to some extent, as well as growth in household income. Meanwhile,
this finding also caused a concern for the affordability issue for low-income households
during this rapid energy switch from free or low-price solid fuels to relatively high-
price clean fuel. In this regard, we think that it is necessary to further discuss the
changes in energy burden and its driving factors during this rapid energy transition
period.
Supplementary Note 5: Connecting energy burden to potential driving factors
Theoretically, drivers of high household energy burden could be roughly divided into
the following five categories: household socioeconomic situation, housing
characteristics, energy prices and policies, location and geographical conditions, and
behavioral factors21,22,23. According to previous studies, considering the availability of
data, this study evaluated a comprehensive set of household-level factors (household
demographic characteristics and socioeconomic status) and a number of distal factors
(gas access penetration, urban-rural settings, and climate conditions) that may be
closely associated with energy burden.
   As shown in Table 1 in the main text, Household-level variables include household
income per capita (Income), the number of household members that live together
(Family_size), the average age of family members (Age). In addition, a dummy variable
Urban, referring to household's living conditions, was also considered into our models,

                                          16 / 19
in order to evaluate the impact of urbanization on household energy burden. Regional-
level variables include the market penetration of gas fuels (Access_gas) and climate
conditions (Climate). Specifically, the value of Access_gas for each region was set by
the provincial statistical data, which is evaluated at the urban and rural scales,
respectively. In addition, the sum of Heating Degree Days (HHD) and Cooling Degree
Days (CDD) for each province was calculated to serve as the value for Climate, which
can be defined as follows24,25,26:

the number of days in the t month; 𝑇𝑘𝑖𝑡 is defined as the daily average temperature in

   Here, we constructed linear and non-linear models to understand which variables, if
any, may correlate to household energy burden. To eliminate bias from unobservable,
including those change over time but are constant over entities, and differences across
households but are constant over provinces and time, the Ordinary Least Squares (OLS)
regression models with household fixed-effects and province-year fixed-effects were
estimated using unbalanced panel. Particularly, province-year fixed-effect model with
clustered standard errors at provincial level was estimated considering that it can
capture any other polices at provincial level that could influence the coefficients
estimated in the empirical analyses.
   Meanwhile, to control the endogeneity, this study incorporated an instrumental
variable (IV), Share_off_farm to the linear model. As shown in Table 1, the indicator
Share_off_farm indicates the share of laborers engaged in nonagricultural employment
in a family. One of reasons for choosing this indicator lies in that we observed that 91.5%
of rural households derived some income from off-farm sources. On average, off-farm
income contributed more than 85% of total income in these households. In this regard,
the share of laborers engaged in nonagricultural employment is likely to closely relate
with household income level, which is unlikely to be affected by individual household
energy burden. To confirm the validity of IV, we conducted correlation test for it, the
results were showed in the Supplementary Table 7. It can be found that this IV
correlated with the core explanatory variable (Income). Finally, the under-identification
and weak-identification tests for the IV was conducted, the results were showed in the
Table 2 in the main text, and the critical values for weak-identification test were presented in
Supplementary Table 7, which implied that the IV was valid.

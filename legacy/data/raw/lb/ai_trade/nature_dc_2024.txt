Abstract
With the rapid development of artificial intelligence, the energy consumption of large-scale data centers continues to grow. Understanding the spatial distribution of large-scale data centers is important for optimizing power system planning, increasing renewable energy penetration, and improving energy efficiency. However, detailed information such as location and distribution of large-scale data centers is rarely available, limiting research on their energy consumption and environmental impacts. Therefore, we present a spatial distribution and environmental attributes dataset of China’s large-scale data centers in 2024. This study integrates Points of Interest from Amap with spatial features extracted from Sentinel-2 imagery and employs the Random Forest model to generate a spatial probability surface for large-scale data centers. The dataset contains the latitude and longitude of 1,005 large-scale data centers in China, urban environmental attributes (climate zone, elevation, annual average temperature, and precipitation), as well as model-generated probability surfaces and satellite image examples. This dataset can be used for estimating energy consumption of artificial intelligence infrastructure, planning and optimizing energy system, and evaluating and enhancing urban energy system resilience.

Background & Summary
Data centers have become essential infrastructure for supporting the rapid expansion of artificial intelligence, while their energy demand is also growing rapidly1. Particularly, in China, with the large-scale deployment of computationally intensive applications, large-scale data centers are continuously expanding2. In 2024, China’s data centers consumed over 166 terawatt-hours (TWh) of electricity, reflecting an annual growth rate of 16.1%3. The electricity consumption of national data centers is projected to reach 525.7 TWh by 2030, equivalent to the annual electricity demand of approximately 350 large-scale aluminum electrolysis plants or that of around 15 million households4. Such rapid growth also amplifies the regional heterogeneity in data center energy demand. Significant differences in energy consumption exist among data centers across different geographical locations, closely related to local environmental conditions such as temperature and humidity5,6. As these factors directly influence cooling loads and operational efficiency, understanding the geographical distribution of large-scale data centers is crucial for accurately assessing their energy demand. This assessment can further support the optimization of power system planning, the increased penetration of renewable energy, and  the improvement of energy efficiency7,8,9. However, information about the specific locations, land occupation, and spatial distribution of large-scale data centers is often not systematically recorded or made publicly available, which constrains accurate assessment of their electricity demand, cooling energy requirements, and environmental impacts.
At present, datasets on the spatial distribution of large-scale data centers are still limited. The existing data are primarily sourced from policy documents, industry reports, and corporate announcements10,11,12. However, this kind of information is often fragmented, non-standardized, and lacks comprehensive, systematic, and reusable datasets at the provincial level. In addition, due to regulatory requirements and other factors, only a portion of data centers can be accessed through the Geographic Information System (GIS) platform, while a large number of data centers do not have publicly available geographic information records. For example, a recent study extracted data center locations from the Gaode point-of-interest (POI) database and administrative records of the Ministry of Industry and Information Technology to analyze how these data centers influence the geographic distribution of AI enterprises13. Although it provides valuable locational data, it is limited to facilities with public administrative registration and lacks detailed physical characteristics, such as rooftop structures and environmental attributes. More importantly, this reliance on public records makes it difficult to identify unpublicized data centers or their potential spatial distribution. Therefore, it is necessary to determine the spatial distribution of these unpublicized data centers through satellite remote sensing identification. Although some datasets have been developed using remote sensing or machine learning techniques to identify industrial parks or specific building types14,15,16, those specifically providing information on data center identification remain scarce. Importantly, industrial infrastructure exhibits visual characteristics that are highly similar to those of data centers, which may lead to potential misidentification. This similarity makes it challenging to precisely classify large-scale data centers and only enables the determination of their spatial probability surfaces.
Therefore, this study constructed a spatial distribution and environmental attributes dataset of China’s large-scale data centers in 2024. This dataset mainly consists of two parts: (1) a verified, georeferenced facility-level dataset of confirmed large-scale data center locations enriched with environmental attributes, and (2) a spatial probability surface that indicates the potential presence of large-scale data centers. Specifically, the locations of large-scale data centers were initially collected and validated by utilizing Gaode POI data17,18. These confirmed facilities were georeferenced and subsequently enriched with environmental attributes, including climate zones, elevation, average annual temperature, and precipitation. Based on this confirmed POI dataset, remote sensing features—including Sentinel-2 spectral bands19,20, nighttime light observations21,22,23, gray level co-occurrence matrix (GLCM) texture metrics, and vegetation and built-up indices24—were extracted to characterize surrounding land-surface conditions. The validated POI locations were then used as positive samples to train a Random Forest (RF) model. The model output was interpreted as a spatial probability surface representing the relative likelihood of large-scale data center presence under different geographic conditions, rather than as a deterministic classification.
Overall, this dataset has the following unique features and potential values. First, the dataset provides 1,005 verified large-scale data center locations collected from Gaode POI, forming a reliable nationwide georeferenced facility-level dataset for model training and spatial analysis. These point-level annotations are essential for validating classification models and investigating the spatial distribution characteristics of large-scale data centers. Second, the dataset includes region-wide remote sensing feature stacks for ten main provincial-level administrative regions. These feature stacks include spectral bands, vegetation and built-up indices, and GLCM-based texture metrics, providing standardized spatial descriptors of land surface conditions and enabling pixel-wise classification, deep learning model development, and remote sensing benchmarking. Third, the dataset provides an RF-generated spatial probability surface representing the relative likelihood of data center presence. These probabilistic surfaces complement the confirmed facility dataset by capturing spatial heterogeneity and highlighting areas with similar siting characteristics. Fourth, the dataset incorporates city-level environmental attributes—such as climate zone classification, elevation, average annual temperature, and annual precipitation—enabling integrated analysis of environmental constraints and geographical drivers influencing the distribution of large-scale data centers.
The dataset can be used to analyze the distribution characteristics of large-scale data centers, thereby facilitating energy consumption estimation and strategic planning for future data center construction in conjunction with environmental conditions. Specifically, it enables a variety of applications, including:
Estimating the energy consumption of artificial intelligence infrastructure. For instance, the electricity demand of large-scale data centers in different regions can be estimated by considering the physical area of the data centers and local environmental conditions.
Planning and optimizing energy system. Identifying the clustering characteristics and spatial distribution differences of large-scale data centers can provide valuable insights for power system planning. The spatial probability surface highlights high-density regions, which—when integrated with environmental attributes and estimated electricity demand—enable a systematic assessment of potential sites for photovoltaic or other renewable energy facilities near data centers.
Evaluating and enhancing urban energy system resilience. The spatial probability surface of large-scale data centers enables the identification of areas with high and concentrated energy loads. By understanding the spatial clustering and operational patterns of large-scale data centers, potential vulnerabilities in urban or regional energy system can be assessed and mitigated, thereby enhancing the overall robustness and reliability of the energy system.
Methods
Workflow overview
The workflow of this study is illustrated in Fig. 1. It consists of three major stages: (1) compilation of confirmed large-scale data center locations, (2) construction of multi-source spatial feature layers, and (3) generation of spatial probability surfaces of large-scale data centers through machine learning modeling. All steps were conducted using the Google Earth Engine (GEE) platform and Quantum GIS (QGIS) software.

Compilation of confirmed large-scale data center locations
POI data have been extensively utilized in remote sensing research since they provide accurate geospatial coordinates and functional categories of human-built facilities25,26,27. These records are typically acquired from online mapping services28, geographic information platforms29, and other similar sources30,31. In this study, we leveraged POI records associated with “data center” retrieved from the Gaode Map platform28 to identify large-scale data centers. To ensure data reliability, all retrieved POIs were manually screened and validated. Duplicate entries, misclassified facilities, and ambiguous records such as small server rooms and unrelated IT offices were removed. In addition, each candidate location was visually inspected using high-resolution satellite imagery to verify the presence of large-scale industrial-type building structures consistent with data center facilities. After undergoing quality control and validation processes, a total of 1,005 confirmed large-scale data center locations were retained. These validated POIs constitute the confirmed facility-level dataset used in this study and serve as positive sample inputs for spatial probability surface modeling. The nationwide spatial distribution of the POI records derived from Gaode maps is shown in Fig. 2.

Construction of multi-source spatial feature layersRemote sensing data sources
Sentinel-2 imagery data
Sentinel-2 provides global multispectral imagery with a 10-m resolution, offering finer spatial detail and fewer mixed-pixel effects compared to the Landsat data of the National Aeronautics and Space Administration (NASA)32. These advantages make it more appropriate for the accurate identification of large-scale data centers. All available cloud-free observations in 2024 were composited by calculating the median reflectance for the visible, near-infrared (NIR), and short-wave infrared (SWIR) bands (B2-B12). The visible and SWIR bands were employed to derive a set of spectral indices, including the normalized difference vegetation index (NDVI), the normalized difference built-up index (NDBI), the modified normalized difference water index (MNDWI), and the SWIR/NIR ratio. These indices capture information about vegetation, built-up structures, and surface moisture conditions33. Moreover, GLCM texture measures extracted from the NIR band characterize the structural regularity typical of large industrial complexes, enabling better discrimination between large-scale data centers and surrounding land uses34,35. Fig. 3 shows an example of the input features extracted from Sentinel-2 satellite images.

Nighttime light intensity
The visible infrared imaging radiometer suite (VIIRS) annual nighttime light composite acts as an indicator of human activity and industrial intensity around large-scale data center clusters36,37. In comparison with optical imagery, nighttime light data can capture stable illumination patterns from industrial facilities, commercial zones, and energy-intensive operations38. By integrating VIIRS radiance, the model can more effectively distinguish large-scale data centers from visually similar but low-activity buildings.
China administrative boundary data
The administrative boundaries of China can be downloaded from the National Geomatics Center of China (NGCC)12. Using standardized administrative units ensures comparability among provincial-level administrative regions and avoids cross-boundary contamination during training and classification. These boundaries also facilitate the production of provincial-level administrative region-specific maps and subsequent spatial analysis. We carefully selected six surface-reflectance bands from Sentinel-2 (B2, B3, B4, B8, B11, B12) to comprehensively capture detailed information across visible, NIR, and SWIR wavelengths. These specific bands are highly effective in differentiating industrial roofs, impervious surfaces, vegetation, and water bodies, which are all commonly found around large-scale data centers.
Subsequently, a set of spectral indices was calculated to improve land-cover separability. Specifically, the NDVI can effectively distinguish vegetated areas from impervious surfaces, as shown in Eq. (1).
$${\rm{NDVI}}=\frac{{\rho }_{B8}-{\rho }_{B4}}{{\rho }_{B8}+{\rho }_{B4}}$$
                    (1)
where \({\rho }_{B8}\) and \({\rho }_{B4}\) represent the surface reflectance of Sentinel-2 B8 (NIR) and B4 (red) bands, respectively.
In contrast, the NDBI highlights built-up structures typically present in industrial campuses, as shown in Eq. (2).
$${\rm{NDBI}}=\frac{{\rho }_{B11}-{\rho }_{B8}}{{\rho }_{B11}+{\rho }_{B8}}$$
                    (2)
where \({\rho }_{B11}\) denotes the reflectance of Sentinel-2 B11 (SWIR) band.
The MNDWI aids in the identification of water bodies and wet surfaces, thereby minimizing false alarms, as shown in Eq. (3).
$${\rm{MNDWI}}=\frac{{\rho }_{B3}-{\rho }_{B11}}{{\rho }_{B3}+{\rho }_{B11}}$$
                    (3)
where \({\rho }_{B3}\) is the reflectance of Sentinel-2 B3 (green) band.
The SWIR/NIR ratio emphasizes material composition and enhances the detection of industrial rooftops, as shown in Eq. (4).
$${\rm{SWIR}}/{\rm{NIR}}=\frac{{\rho }_{B11}}{{\rho }_{B8}}$$
                    (4)
These indices complement the raw spectral reflectance by amplifying the functional and material differences among land-cover types.
Additionally, given that large-scale data centers typically comprise large rectangular buildings with highly regular surface features, five GLCM texture metrics were extracted to capture spatial structural characteristics. All GLCM calculations were standardized using well-defined parameters (Table 3).

where \({\rm{Contrast}}\) measures local intensity variation, \({\rm{Entropy}}\) quantifies texture randomness, \(\varepsilon ={10}^{-6}\) avoids \(\log (0)\), \({\rm{Variance}}\) reflects gray-level dispersion around the mean \(\mu \), \({\rm{Homogeneity}}\) is the Inverse Difference Moment, indicates surface uniformity, and \({\rm{Dissimilarity}}\) measures adjacent pixel gray-level differences. In addition, \(P(i,j)\) represents the joint probability of a pixel with gray level \(i\) being adjacent to a pixel with gray level \(j\) within the 3 × 3 calculation window.
These texture descriptors capture the spatial variations in tone and surface uniformity, thus providing robust metrics for identifying large-scale built-up structures39,40.
Furthermore, VIIRS nighttime radiance data (500-m resolution, resampled to 10-m via bilinear interpolation) were incorporated to characterize the stable illumination features associated with large-scale industrial and digital infrastructure facilities. This characteristic helps distinguish large-scale data centers from low-activity industrial warehouses41.
All features were resampled to a consistent 10-m spatial resolution, geospatially aligned to the same coordinate system (WGS84/UTM Zone 50 N), and normalized to the range \([0,1]\) to support machine learning classification. The final feature stack integrated 6 spectral bands, 4 spectral indices, 5 texture metrics, and 1 nighttime light variable, resulting in a 16-dimensional feature space for subsequent RF model input.
Generation of spatial probability surfaces of large-scale data centers
A RF model was applied to generate a nationwide spatial probability surface of data center presence. This model complements the POI-based confirmed data center locations by identifying potential, unrecorded data centers that are not included in public POI datasets, thereby producing a more complete spatial distribution dataset.Sample construction
The 1,005 validated POI data center locations were used as positive samples. To represent heterogeneous background conditions and reduce false positives, 2,010 negative samples were collected from diverse land-cover and built-up types, including vegetated areas (forests, grasslands, croplands), water bodies, bare land, and urban surfaces. Moreover, non-data center industrial facilities like logistics parks, warehouses, manufacturing plants, and commercial buildings were incorporated to enhance the discriminability between data centers and visually similar structures.
In total, 3,015 samples were used for model training. The spatial distribution of the positive and negative samples is shown in Fig. 4.

Full size image
Spatial distribution of positive and negative training samples for large-scale data centers across China.Regional probabilistic modeling
The primary reason for selecting the RF model for large-scale data center identification is its robust ability to model complex, nonlinear relationships in high-dimensional feature spaces42. Moreover, RF has been extensively and reliably employed in remote sensing tasks, such as built-up area mapping, industrial-facility detection, and land-use classification. RF has demonstrated good performance in the presence of noise and with limited training samples43,44,45.
We implemented the RF classifiers using the GEE Application Programming Interface (API)46,47,48. Unlike conventional hard classification which assigns binary labels (0 or 1), the RF classifier in GEE can be configured to output class probabilities by setting the output mode parameter to “Probability”. In this configuration, each pixel value represents the proportion of decision trees voting for the positive class. This probabilistic output allows for a spatially continuous representation of the likelihood of data centers instead of discrete classification.
Given that the study covers multiple provincial-level administrative regions across China, data centers exhibit significant visual and contextual variability due to differences in land use and siting environments. For example, data centers in highly urbanized regions such as Beijing or Guangdong are usually embedded within dense commercial or industrial complexes. In contrast, those in provincial-level administrative regions such as Inner Mongolia or Guizhou are frequently located in suburban industrial parks with more open surroundings. These regional disparities pose challenges for training a single nationwide model that can consistently achieve high accuracy.
Considering regional heterogeneity, we employed a provincial-level, region-specific modeling approach, training individual RF classifiers for each province14. This regional modeling strategy effectively reduces intra-class variability, enhances the discriminative ability of spectral and textural features, and improves spatial consistency of the resulting probability surfaces17,20. Ten provincial-level administrative regions with sufficient validated data center samples were selected as modeling units. These regions collectively account for approximately 60% of all validated large-scale data centers in China. For each provincial-level administrative region, samples were randomly split into training and validation subsets in a 7:3 ratio—a common and widely accepted practice in remote sensing and machine learning classification studies14,15. Model performance was evaluated using precision, recall, and F1 score to ensure reliability of the probability estimates.Cross validation
To assess spatial transferability, each provincial model was tested using samples from the remaining provinces in a pairwise cross-validation framework. This procedure provides an evaluation of model generalizability under heterogeneous geographic conditions and supports the robustness of the probability surface generation approach. Similarly, precision, recall, and F1 score were used as performance metrics to ensure consistency and comparability across assessments. Moreover, the cross-regional evaluation framework directly assesses the generalizability of each provincial model beyond its training region. If there were substantial overfitting to local characteristics, the performance would significantly decline when the model is applied to other provinces.Active learning
Generating highly accurate spatial probability maps within a single iteration poses significant challenges, especially when the number of labeled samples is restricted. Therefore, we employed an active learning strategy to iteratively improve model performance. An initial RF model was trained using POI-validated samples from ten provincial-level administrative regions, producing preliminary probability outputs. Regions exhibiting misclassifications or low prediction confidence were identified and manually inspected to collect additional high-quality training samples. Subsequently, the model was then retrained using these augmented samples, and this iterative process was repeated until the performance metrics reached a stable state. After the final iteration, the optimized provincial RF classifiers were applied to the corresponding feature stacks to generate pixel-wise probability values ranging from 0 to 1. The resulting probability raster represents the model-estimated likelihood of large-scale data center presence and constitutes the probabilistic surface component of the dataset.
Data Records
The dataset provides a spatial distribution and environmental attributes dataset of China’s large-scale data centers in 2024, integrating validated POI records, RF model-derived spatial probability surfaces, and satellite imagery. It consists of three components: (1) verified POI locations of large-scale data centers across all 31 provincial-level administrative regions; (2) probability surfaces generated by RF models for ten provincial-level administrative regions that collectively hold approximately 60% of all known large-scale data centers in China; and (3) ten representative satellite images. Provincial-level feature calculation results are provided in component (2) of the dataset. This dataset complements confirmed POI locations with model-predicted probabilistic surfaces, effectively addressing the lack of publicly available geographic records for some large-scale data centers due to regulatory supervision requirement. All components of the dataset are openly and permanently accessible via the Science Data Bank (https://doi.org/10.57760/sciencedb.32970)49, with a unified geospatial projection (WGS84/UTM Zone 50 N) and a temporal baseline (2024). The source code is available on GitHub (https://github.com/ziweiyang1206/data-center-classification.git)50. Verified POI dataset of large-scale data centers
A comma-separated value (CSV) file contains verified large-scale data center records. Unlike the dataset provided by Ref. 13, which focuses primarily on locational coordinates for industrial layout analysis, this study offers multi-dimensional characterization of each facility. Specifically, this CSV file integrates provincial-level administrative region, city, longitude, latitude, average annual temperature, average annual precipitation, elevation, climate zone, and facility types, all of which are free of missing values. Climatic and topographic attributes were spatially linked to POI geolocations via point-to-raster spatial interpolation, consistent with the 2024 temporal baseline. Spatial probability surface for large-scale data centers
This component consists of ten provincial-level administrative region-specific folders, each corresponding to one of the ten representative provinces. Each folder contains a high-resolution GeoTIFF raster file, a QGIS project file, and a metadata file. The GeoTIFF has a 10-m spatial resolution. Each raster file stores pixel values that represent the predicted probability of large-scale data centers (range: 0-1).
Satellite images
This component includes ten high-resolution satellite images that were manually captured from Google Earth high-resolution satellite imagery in 2024 with a spatial resolution of 0.5 m to 2 m. Each image corresponds to a typical large-scale data center within the provincial-level administrative region and reflects key visual characteristics, including building morphology, roof structure, and cooling system layout. Moreover, each image records the geographic coordinates (WGS84) of the data center and quantifies the facility footprint via Google Earth Pro measurement tool, with area results reported in square meters. The full set of annotated satellite imagery is hosted at a dedicated persistent Google Earth link (https://earth.google.com/earth/d/1Wiu-2pyGJs3lt6NVdQoTr_nZLsbBUxzL?usp = sharing) for interactive visualization and online access.
Technical Validation
Feature importance analysis
The RF model provides feature importance scores, enabling direct interpretability of the model. To validate this advantage, we calculated the feature importance of 16 variables for each of the ten provincial-level administrative regions. All importance scores were normalized to ensure comparability of each feature’s relative contribution across regions. The normalized provincial-level scores were then averaged to obtain a national-scale ranking (Fig. 6).

(k) Proportion of feature importance at the national scale. (l) Proportion of different feature types in ten provincial-level administrative regions.
Fig. 6a–j shows the normalized feature importance for each province. Nighttime light consistently ranks as the most important feature, often contributing more than 20% in some provinces. Key spectral bands (B8, B4, and B11) and index-based features (NDBI and MNDWI) also contribute meaningfully, highlighting their stable effectiveness in capturing built-up areas and characterizing large-scale data center infrastructure and associated impervious surfaces. Texture features, especially variance and homogeneity, further support classification by encoding spatial characteristics that are distinctive to these facilities.
In addition, we conducted a lightweight ablation experiment using only the nighttime light feature (Table 6). The results indicate that nighttime light alone achieves a substantial F1 score (0.7819), but overall performance decreases compared with the full feature set (0.8875). This confirms that, while nighttime light is highly informative, the combination with spectral, index, and texture features is essential to robustly distinguish large-scale data centers.

Accuracy assessment
To comprehensively evaluate the reliability of the probability-based large-scale data center classification derived from multispectral satellite imagery, we conducted systematic accuracy assessments in the ten selected provincial-level administrative regions. The evaluation metrics encompass the Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve (AUC), Accuracy, Precision, Recall, and F1 score (Fig. 7). Overall, the results consistently show strong classification performance, which validates the robustness and general applicability of the RF model in diverse geographic and environmental contexts.
Fig. 7
Full size image
Model performance in ten provincial-level administrative regions.
As shown in Fig. 7, most provinces achieved Accuracy values above 0.86. Jiangsu (0.95), Sichuan (0.92), and Inner Mongolia (0.985) exhibited particularly strong performance. These results reflect the model’s outstanding adaptability to both highly urbanized regions like Beijing and Guangdong and environmentally heterogeneous landscapes such as Guizhou and Hebei. Precision values generally exceeded 0.80 across all provincial-level administrative regions. Regions such as Anhui (0.857), Guizhou (0.909), and Beijing (0.913) exhibited excellent precision in controlling false positives, indicating that the classifier rarely misidentifies non-data center features as data centers. In contrast, Guangdong (0.828) and Hebei (0.765) showed relatively lower Precision, primarily due to spectral similarity between industrial buildings and other large artificial surfaces. Recall remained consistently high, with multiple provincial-level administrative regions achieving or exceeding 0.90. This demonstrates that the model can identify true data center pixels with a high probability, even in areas with mixed land cover. Even in provinces with comparatively lower Recall like Anhui (0.800) and Shandong (0.80), detection performance remained stable. The F1 score, balancing Precision and Recall, exceeded 0.83 in all provinces. Inner Mongolia (0.966), Jiangsu (0.958), and Sichuan (0.923) achieved the highest F1 scores, reflecting balanced and robust classification performance based on predicted probabilities.
To rigorously evaluate model generalization and assess the necessity of provincial-level modeling, we trained a national-scale model and compared its F1 scores with those of province-specific models. We found that training on data from all provinces does not improve the overall F1 score. Instead, the national-scale model yields a lower average F1 score of 0.8276 compared to 0.8875 achieved by province-specific models. Furthermore, performance variation across provinces is heterogeneous: the national-scale model achieves comparable or slightly higher F1 scores only in provinces with similar geomorphic and built-up environments such as Guangdong and Hebei, whereas its performance declines in regions with distinct environmental characteristics such as Inner Mongolia. These results confirm that provincial-level stratified modeling effectively reduces regional bias and avoids overfitting, thereby validating our modeling strategy.
The Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curves for the ten provincial-level administrative regions further demonstrate the strong probabilistic discriminative capability of the RF model (Fig. 8).

The ROC curves (Fig. 8a–j), which are constructed based on predicted probability scores rather than binary outputs, indicate that the RF model achieves strong discriminative performance across all ten provinces. In each province, the ROC curve lies substantially above the 1:1 reference line, implying that high predicted probabilities are consistently associated with the actual locations of data centers, while non–data center areas are assigned comparatively low probability scores. Regions such as Inner Mongolia (AUC = 0.99), Shandong (AUC = 0.97), and Jiangsu (AUC = 0.95) have ROC curves approaching the upper-left corner, which reflects extremely robust classification performance. In contrast, regions with more heterogeneous land-use structures, including Anhui (AUC = 0.84), Hubei (AUC = 0.84), and Hebei (AUC = 0.88), show slightly flatter curves. Nevertheless, their ROC curves remain smooth and strongly convex, suggesting stable probabilistic ranking performance even in the face of mixed industrial, agricultural, and residential background conditions. Fig. 8k provides an overall summary of the AUC values with the 95% confidence interval. The provincial AUCs range from 0.84 to 0.99, with an average of 0.91, demonstrating consistently strong probabilistic discrimination across diverse geographic and socioeconomic conditions. The relatively narrow confidence interval further suggests robust generalization ability of the model.
Cross validation
To further assess the spatial transferability of provincial RF models under heterogeneous environmental conditions, we conducted a cross-regional transfer experiment. For each of the ten provinces, a RF model was trained using its own feature image and a balanced set of positive and negative samples. The trained model was then applied to the remaining nine provinces to generate probability predictions of data center presence, resulting in a 10 × 10 performance matrix (excluding the diagonal). For each train-test pair, probability outputs were converted into binary predictions using a consistent threshold, and accuracy, precision, recall, and F1 score were calculated based on balanced validation samples. To summarize transferability, we averaged the F1 scores of each trained model across the nine provinces in which it was externally tested (Fig. 9).

Cross-regional transfer performance of the RF-based data center identification model. (a–d) Accuracy, Precision, Recall, and F1 score under inter-provincial model transfer, respectively.
The cross-regional analysis reveals significant spatial variation in model generalizability. Models trained in Guizhou, Hubei, Hebei, and Shandong, exhibit relatively strong transferability, with the mean cross-regional F1 scores ranging from 0.80 to 0.81. This suggests that the spectral-textural feature patterns learned in these provinces capture the morphological characteristics of large-scale data centers that are widely shared across multiple regions.
In contrast, models trained in provinces with highly distinctive environmental contexts, especially Inner Mongolia, show weaker transferability, with an average cross-regional F1 score of 0.684. Furthermore, models trained in other provinces perform poorly when transferred to Inner Mongolia. This is mainly due to the fact that large-scale data centers in Inner Mongolia are often located in sparsely built or industrial-desert transitional landscapes, whereas those in many eastern provinces are embedded within dense urban or peri-urban built-up environments. The divergence in background spectral and spatial patterns reduces inter-regional model compatibility. The cross-regional transfer experiment confirms that although provincial RF models exhibit stable probabilistic discrimination within their training domains and transfer well across provinces with comparable land-cover structures, domain heterogeneity remains a key constraint on nationwide generalization. These findings support a region-specific training strategy rather than reliance on a single, unified national model.
Overall, the observed regional performance differences in this study highlight the influence of spatial heterogeneity and uncertainty in reference data on probabilistic discrimination. The validation results demonstrate robust province-specific model performance and consistent cross-regional transferability, providing a transparent assessment of dataset reliability across diverse environmental contexts.
Usage Notes
Ground-reference locations were collected from publicly available POI records and visually verified using satellite imagery. These points indicate reported facility locations rather than exact building footprints, so minor positional uncertainty may exist. Users requiring precise delineation should integrate high-resolution imagery or local data sources. The provided probabilistic maps represent the likelihood of large-scale data center presence—not binary classifications. Probability thresholds should be chosen according to specific analytical requirements. Model evaluation shows strong within-province performance, but cross-regional comparability may be affected by geographic heterogeneity. Users should take regional differences into account when interpreting probabilities. Example satellite images illustrate typical facility configurations and can serve as visual reference, training, or support the training or validation of automated detection models.
